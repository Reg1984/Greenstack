The UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) has been law since the Finance Act 2026 received Royal Assent in March 2026. It starts on **1 January 2027**. If your organisation imports iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, or hydrogen products above a £50,000 threshold, you have about six weeks to get ready for a liability that will begin accruing on New Year's Day.

This guide explains what CBAM is, who it catches, how the charge works, and — critically — why the data collection work has to happen in 2027, not 2028.

## Why CBAM exists

UK manufacturers have paid a carbon price under the UK Emissions Trading Scheme (UK ETS) for years. Importers historically haven't. That gap undermines both domestic producers and the UK's emissions targets: if it's cheaper to import a carbon-intensive product than to make it domestically under a carbon price, production — and emissions — simply shift abroad. CBAM levels the playing field by applying a comparable carbon charge to imported goods.

The EU runs a parallel scheme for the same reason, though the two regimes work differently. As things stand, there is no UK-EU ETS linkage agreement in force, which means **EU-origin goods are chargeable under UK CBAM just like goods from any other country** — a point many importers have missed.

## Who is caught

Three conditions determine whether UK CBAM applies to you:

1. **You are the importer of record.** The liable person is the business named on the customs declaration. Not the overseas producer, not your freight forwarder.
2. **You import covered goods.** The five sectors are iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, and hydrogen. The scope reaches further downstream than many expect: screws, bolts, aluminium doors and structures, nitric acid, and ammonia are all included. Scrap is not. Goods are defined by 8-digit commodity code.
3. **You cross the £50,000 threshold.** This is measured as the customs value of covered goods on two tests: a rolling 12-month look-back checked on the first day of each month, and a forward-looking test for any 30-day window expected to exceed £50,000. Meeting either triggers registration.

The government's own analysis says the £50,000 threshold keeps over 99% of imported emissions in scope while excluding over 80% of businesses that would otherwise be caught. It is designed to catch mid-sized importers, not just multinationals.

## How the charge is calculated

The formula is straightforward:

**CBAM charge = tonnes of embodied CO₂e × sectoral CBAM rate − carbon price relief**

### Embodied emissions

The charge covers the **direct emissions** from producing the imported good, including emissions from precursor materials (the steel in fasteners, the clinker in cement, the ammonia in fertiliser). Indirect emissions from electricity are excluded until at least 2029 — a deliberate simplification.

For each import line, you have two options:

- **Actual supplier data** — an independently verified emissions intensity figure from the producer's installation, measured over a calendar monitoring period. Producers already reporting for EU CBAM will find the UK methodology similar, though not identical.
- **Default values** — HMRC published these on 16 October 2026, one per 8-digit commodity code, the same regardless of country of origin, in force for 2027 and 2028. They are built from EU data weighted to UK imports, with a **10% markup applied in every sector except fertiliser** — meaning defaults are deliberately set higher than actual data, to incentivise supplier engagement.

One rule makes this choice consequential: **once a return is filed using default values, it cannot be amended to substitute actual data**. Whatever data you fail to collect during 2027 stays uncollected.

### CBAM rates (illustrative, as of October 2026)

HMRC published illustrative sector rates on 16 October 2026. The official Q1 2027 rates are due before 31 December 2026.

| Sector | Illustrative rate (per tonne CO₂e) |
|---|---|
| Iron and steel | £9.69 |
| Aluminium | £9.79 |
| Cement | £8.78 |
| Hydrogen | £9.24 |
| Fertiliser | £1.45 |

These rates are substantially below the current UK ETS price because UK producers still receive most of their free allowances. That free allocation phases out over time, so CBAM rates will rise in subsequent years.

### Carbon price relief

If the imported goods were produced in a country with its own qualifying carbon pricing mechanism, the effective carbon price already paid can be deducted from the UK charge — capped at the liability itself, and subject to independent verification. This is not an exemption for specific countries: the relief is available wherever a qualifying carbon price was actually paid and can be evidenced.

## Every deadline that matters

| Date | What it means |
|---|---|
| Before 31 December 2026 | Official Q1 2027 CBAM rates published — use these to budget your liability |
| **1 January 2027** | CBAM liability starts accruing on all covered imports |
| 1 January 2028 | HMRC registration service opens |
| **31 January 2028** | Registration deadline for anyone who crossed the threshold in 2027 |
| **31 May 2028** | First return and full payment due, covering all of 2027 |
| From 2028 | Quarterly accounting periods with rolling deadlines |
| 2029 (earliest) | Indirect (electricity) emissions may be brought into scope |

The trap in this timeline is that it appears as if nothing happens until 2028. The opposite is true. **The return you file in May 2028 is built entirely from data you collect during 2027** — commodity codes, weights, origins, customs values, and supplier emissions intensity figures complete with their monitoring periods and verifier credentials. 2027 is the work. 2028 is just the paperwork.

## What to do in the next six weeks

**1. Establish whether you're in scope.** Pull a 12-month import report from your customs broker or freight forwarder filtered by commodity code and cross-reference it against the covered goods list. The £50,000 threshold applies to customs value, not selling price.

**2. Estimate your liability.** Use the illustrative sector rates published on 16 October 2026 as a budgeting basis until the official Q1 2027 rates land. Your customs data, combined with HMRC's published default values, gives you a credible order-of-magnitude figure now.

**3. Start the supplier conversation.** You can use default values as a fallback, but actual supplier emissions data will almost always be lower — and once you file on defaults, you can't go back. The earlier you open this conversation, the more time suppliers have to arrange independent verification. Many suppliers in scope for the EU CBAM will already have verified data; ask what they have.

**4. Set up a data collection process for 1 January.** Per-line commodity codes, net weights, country of origin, customs values, and supplier emissions intensity — with monitoring-period dates and verifier details — need to be captured for every covered shipment from day one. This is an operational change, not a year-end accounting task.

**5. Put the registration date in the diary.** HMRC's registration service opens on 1 January 2028. The deadline to register is 31 January 2028. Don't wait until then: registering promptly will make the May 2028 return straightforward.

## The bigger picture

CBAM is part of a wider tightening of carbon accountability for importers and manufacturers. The Transition Plan Taskforce disclosure framework, mandatory climate-related financial disclosures for large companies, and ongoing UK ETS expansion all point in the same direction: the carbon embedded in supply chains is becoming a financial and reporting reality, not just a sustainability aspiration.

For procurement teams, finance directors, and sustainability leads at organisations that import covered goods, the question is no longer whether CBAM applies — it is whether your data infrastructure and supplier relationships are ready for a regime that starts accruing liability in 47 days.
