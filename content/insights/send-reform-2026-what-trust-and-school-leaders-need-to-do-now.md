The Government's Schools White Paper, *Every Child Achieving and Thriving*, published in February 2026, marks the most ambitious overhaul of SEND support in England since the Children and Families Act 2014. For trust leaders, headteachers and local authority SEND teams, the autumn term is exactly the right moment to take stock of what is coming, what it means in practice, and where the pressure points lie.

## The core shift: mainstream inclusion as the default

The white paper's central proposition is that far more children with SEND should have their needs met in mainstream settings, supported by better-resourced schools rather than routed by default into specialist or independent provision. The Government has committed £1.6 billion over three years from 2026/27 specifically to strengthen the mainstream inclusion offer — a significant investment, but one that arrives alongside an expectation of real structural change.

The most visible commitment is that every secondary school in England should have an **inclusion base** — a dedicated space providing specialist in-school support. The Government has also set an ambition for many primary schools to follow suit. As of spring 2026, the DfE acknowledged it lacked reliable national data on what specialist facilities already exist, and analysis by the TES revealed significant regional gaps. For trust leaders with secondary schools that do not yet have a dedicated inclusion base, this is something to begin planning for now.

## The new tiered support model

The white paper proposes replacing the current single-tier SEN Support approach with a **four-tier model**: universal, targeted, targeted-plus, and specialist. The intention is to make clearer what level of support each pupil is receiving and what the school's responsibilities are at each stage.

Alongside this, the Government proposes **Individual Support Plans (ISPs)** — digital records for every child or young person with identified SEND, whether or not they hold an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP). ISPs will sit at the targeted and targeted-plus tiers, with schools expected to create and maintain them as a matter of course. A national, evidence-based digital library of resources to support implementation of the National Inclusion Standard is expected to be in place by 2028.

What this means in practice is that SENDCos and class teachers will need stronger shared systems for tracking, reviewing and acting on individual support data. Schools that already have well-embedded graduated approaches will be better placed to transition; those that rely on ad hoc arrangements or overstretched SENDCo capacity will face real implementation challenges.

## EHCPs: no changes before September 2030

One significant source of anxiety in the sector has been the future of Education, Health and Care Plans. The Government has been clear: **there will be no changes to EHCPs before at least September 2030**. Children and young people with existing EHCPs will retain them. The reforms focus on strengthening what happens before the EHCP threshold, not on removing existing entitlements.

This matters for how schools and local authorities communicate with families. The Disabled Children's Partnership's consultation response emphasised that existing rights must be protected and strengthened, and many parent groups remain watchful. Trust and local authority leaders should be transparent with families about what is changing and what is not.

## The funding picture: a new local SEND inclusion formula

The financial backdrop to all of this is severe. The Local Government Association estimated the cumulative high needs deficit across local authorities had reached approximately **£3.15 billion**, threatening the financial viability of some councils. The DfE responded in 2026/27 by making a High Needs Stability Grant available, covering 90% of high needs-related DSG deficits accrued to the end of March 2026. This is significant relief for many authorities, but it does not resolve the underlying structural pressure.

On the funding model itself, a consultation published in July 2026 — *Upfront funding for mainstream schools: creating a local SEND inclusion formula* — closed in September 2026. The proposal explored how local authorities can increase the share of high needs funding that flows directly to mainstream schools as upfront, formula-based allocations, rather than being held centrally and deployed through referral and placement decisions. The intent is to shift financial incentives so that mainstream inclusion becomes more viable — but the detail of how any formula will work nationally, and how transition funding will be managed, remains to be confirmed.

For school finance leads and trust CFOs, the key implication is that **the funding landscape for SEND support is shifting** — both in how money flows to schools and in what schools are expected to do with it.

## What the new SEND Code of Practice will mean for governance

The white paper commits to a new SEND Code of Practice, backed by a £4 billion investment package over four years. The current Code (2015) will eventually be replaced, though legislation is required and the timetable for a fully revised Code is not yet confirmed. Until then, the 2015 Code remains statutory guidance and schools and local authorities are legally required to follow it.

For governors and trustees, this is a moment to review:

- Whether SEND is a standing item on governing body agendas, with meaningful data and progress reporting
- Whether the trust or school's self-evaluation reflects current SEND provision honestly
- Whether SENDCo capacity is sufficient given rising demand and the coming reforms
- Whether the governing body understands how the local SEND inclusion formula consultation might affect future funding

## Practical steps for this term

The reforms are moving through consultation and legislation — but the direction is clear, and the operational preparation cannot wait for final regulations. Here is what leaders can usefully do now:

- **Map your current inclusion provision** against the four-tier model. Identify where your graduated approach is strong and where it is inconsistent across year groups or subjects.
- **Audit SENDCo capacity and workload.** The new ISP requirement, if legislated, will add to administrative load. Consider whether time allocation and administrative support are adequate.
- **Engage with your local authority's SEND reform planning.** Local authorities are required to submit local SEND reform plans to the DfE. Schools and trusts should be part of that conversation, not recipients of decisions made elsewhere.
- **Start the conversation about inclusion bases.** For secondary schools without a designated base, begin scoping what space, staffing and specialist input would be needed — and what capital or revenue funding might be available.
- **Review how you communicate with families of children with SEND.** With significant policy change comes uncertainty. Proactive, honest communication builds trust; reactive communication during a crisis does not.

## The bigger picture

The SEND reform agenda reflects a genuine effort to address a system that has been under severe financial and operational strain for years. The ambitions are real: better outcomes for children, more sustainable use of specialist provision, and a mainstream sector that can genuinely meet a wider range of needs. But the gap between ambition and implementation is where most education reform runs into difficulty — and the history of SEND policy in England offers several cautionary examples.

Trust and school leaders, local authority SEND teams and education charities all have a role in making reform work in practice. The evidence base on what effective SEND inclusion looks like — in terms of staffing, training, pedagogy and funding — exists. The task now is to apply it systematically, at scale, before the legislative changes arrive.
