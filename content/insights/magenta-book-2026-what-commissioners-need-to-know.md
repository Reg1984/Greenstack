In May 2026, HM Treasury and the Cabinet Office Evaluation Task Force (ETF) published [a substantially revised Magenta Book](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book) — the UK government's central guidance on how public programmes should be evaluated. The ETF described it as "the most significant revision in standards since 2020." For anyone commissioning, managing or reporting on evaluation work, it's worth understanding what changed and what it asks of you.

## Six things that are genuinely new

The 2026 update introduced six new or significantly expanded areas. Each has practical consequences.

**1. Test and Learn gets its own annex**

The previous version acknowledged that evaluation could be formative (during delivery) or summative (at the end). The 2026 revision goes much further, adding a dedicated annex that positions Test and Learn as a way of working, not just an evaluation method. The idea is that programmes should build in social research and iterative learning from the start, so that evidence can shape delivery in real time — not just justify decisions in retrospect.

For commissioners, this changes what a good evaluation plan looks like at the design stage. Building in mechanisms for early learning is now explicitly expected, especially for complex or uncertain programmes. If you're commissioning a new service and your evaluation plan only describes an end-of-programme impact study, the Magenta Book is asking you to think again.

**2. Value for money has been redrafted and expanded**

The VfM section has been substantially reworked to draw on both HM Treasury Green Book and National Audit Office frameworks. Crucially, it now explicitly addresses outcomes that cannot easily be monetised. The guidance is clear that value for money is a judgement, not just a benefit–cost ratio, and that evaluators should make a rounded assessment — including whether strategic objectives were met.

For charities, councils and third-sector providers, this matters. Many programmes generate community cohesion, improved wellbeing or long-term opportunity that is hard to put a figure on. The updated guidance gives you more legitimate methodological ground to stand on when assessing — and asserting — that value. It also references Social Return on Investment (SROI) alongside the Four Es (economy, efficiency, effectiveness, equity) as valid analytical frameworks.

**3. Research transparency: pre-registration is now guidance**

The 2026 revision introduces transparency principles new to the Magenta Book. These include recommending that evaluation protocols be pre-registered before data collection begins, and that code and analysis be shared on open platforms such as GitHub. This brings government evaluation guidance in line with what academic and third-sector evaluators have increasingly been doing.

Pre-registration matters because it separates hypotheses from findings — you can't be accused of moving the goalposts if your theory of change and primary outcomes are on record before you start. For commissioners procuring external evaluation, it's worth asking prospective evaluators whether they intend to pre-register and how they plan to publish their materials.

**4. AI in evaluation: cautionary principles embedded throughout**

There is now a dedicated section on the responsible and ethical use of artificial intelligence in social research and evaluation (section 5.6), with AI-related considerations also embedded in broader methodological discussions. The guidance does not prohibit AI tools — rather, it frames a set of questions evaluators should ask about quality, transparency, accuracy and bias before deploying them.

This is relevant for commissioners because AI-assisted tools are increasingly appearing in evidence synthesis, literature reviews and data analysis. Knowing that the Magenta Book now sets out principles for their responsible use gives you a framework for scrutinising what your evaluators are doing — and for asking the right questions in procurement.

**5. Place-based evaluation gets its own section**

Place-based policymaking is high on the agenda: devolution, combined authorities, Area-Based Curriculum, the Growth Mission — all of them involve interventions designed for specific geographies. The 2026 Magenta Book recognises this with a new section (2.3) dedicated to evaluating place-based programmes.

It's honest about the difficulties: establishing a credible counterfactual for a local initiative is genuinely hard. Spillover and displacement effects (did the programme just shift a problem to a neighbouring area?) are notoriously difficult to quantify. For commissioners of place-based work — combined authority analysts, local public health teams, regional funders — the guidance offers practical considerations without pretending the methodological problems don't exist. That candour is useful.

**6. Evaluation and benefits management: finally distinguished**

A persistent source of confusion in programme delivery is the difference between benefits management (a project management discipline focused on realising expected outcomes during delivery) and evaluation (a systematic, evidence-based assessment of what happened and why). The updated Magenta Book includes new content that explicitly maps the relationship between the two and explains where they differ.

This matters for commissioners who sit at the boundary of programme delivery and policy accountability — programme managers, grant managers and performance leads in particular. The guidance makes it clearer that benefits tracking within a programme does not substitute for independent evaluation.

## What this asks of commissioners

The 2026 Magenta Book is explicitly designed for a broader audience than its predecessors. Its foreword describes it as "a shared roadmap across professions" — for policymakers, analysts, delivery professionals and finance specialists together. That framing is deliberate.

A few practical implications:

- **Evaluation should be scoped at business-case stage**, not commissioned as an afterthought once a programme is running. The revised guidance strengthens the link between evaluation and business cases. If your programme business case doesn't reference an evaluation plan, that's a gap.
- **Commissioners should ask for pre-registration.** For significant programmes, requiring your evaluator to pre-register their protocol is now consistent with central guidance.
- **VfM evidence is broader than it was.** If your programme generates social outcomes that resist monetisation, you have more methodological options — and more explicit backing from guidance — to present a rounded assessment.
- **Place-based programmes need bespoke evaluation design.** Don't retrofit a standard logic model; think about counterfactuals, spillovers and local context from the start.

## Where to read it

The full updated Magenta Book is available on [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book). It aligns with the updated Green Book published in the same period. The [Civil Service blog post from 15 May 2026](https://civilservice.blog.gov.uk/2026/05/15/major-shift-in-government-evaluation-as-magenta-book-is-updated/) gives a good summary of the ETF's intentions. GLA Economics has also published a [detailed commentary](https://data.london.gov.uk/blog/he-new-magenta-book-improving-evaluation-in-government) worth reading alongside the guidance itself.
