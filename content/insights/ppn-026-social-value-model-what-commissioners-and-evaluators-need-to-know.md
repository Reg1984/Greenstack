With fewer than seven weeks until the new Procurement Policy Note 026 (PPN 026) takes effect, many teams commissioning or bidding for central government contracts are still working out what the changes actually mean for how they design, measure and evidence social value. This piece sets out the key facts, the practical challenges, and what good social value measurement looks like under the new model.

## What has changed — and what it means in practice

PPN 026 — published by the Cabinet Office in August 2026, with detailed implementation guidance following on 28 October 2026 — replaces PPN 06/20 as the governing framework for social value in central government procurement. It applies to covered central government contracts (departments, executive agencies, NDPBs) worth £1m or more including VAT, commenced on or after 1 January 2027.

The headline change is on weighting. The minimum social value weighting stays at **10%** for contracts between £1m and £5m, but it doubles to a **minimum of 20%** for contracts worth £5m or more. For large infrastructure, construction or programme delivery contracts, that means social value now carries comparable weight to major technical delivery criteria. A thin or unconvincing social value answer will cost points that cannot be recovered elsewhere.

The model restructures social value around two outcomes and six Model Award Criteria (MACs):

| Outcome | Model Award Criteria |
|---------|---------------------|
| Good jobs | 1a: Create and maintain good jobs; 1b: Adapted recruitment and retention; 1c: Fair pay and working conditions |
| Skills and opportunities | 2a: Remove barriers to employment; 2b: Develop skills; 2c: Strengthen communities |

Contracts of £5m or more must have at least one social value KPI published on the Central Digital Platform (Find a Tender). Buyers are encouraged to set a KPI on smaller contracts too. KPIs must use Standard Reporting Metrics (SRMs) where they exist — the guidance lists 37 SRMs, covering quantitative measures such as number of jobs created, number of training weeks delivered, and number of work placements offered.

## Why measurement is now the hard part

Under PPN 006/20, buyers often evaluated social value through broad commitments scored against a checklist. PPN 026 moves the emphasis: at tender stage, evaluation is **qualitative by default**, scoring the convincingness and plausibility of the method statement, not the size of numerical promises. But during contract delivery, suppliers are held accountable through the agreed KPIs, using the SRMs.

This creates a gap that many organisations — both buyers and suppliers — are not yet ready for:

- **Buyers** need to decide which MACs are most relevant to their contract, set KPIs that are proportionate and measurable, and build monitoring and verification into contract management from day one.
- **Suppliers** need to design delivery models that genuinely produce the social value they claim, and to have data collection systems that can evidence SRM progress throughout the contract.
- **Evaluators** brought in to assess programme impact need to understand how social value commitments were specified, what data the supplier collected, and how to attribute outcomes to the programme rather than to wider labour market or skills trends.

The Cabinet Office guidance warns buyers against accepting large-quantity pledges that cannot be substantiated. If a supplier promises 45 work experience weeks in a method statement, the KPI mechanism is what actually holds them to it. Without a credible measurement plan behind the commitment, neither buyer nor supplier is in a strong position.

## What good social value evaluation looks like

There is no single template, but robust social value evaluation under PPN 026 should cover several elements.

**A clear theory of change.** Which MACs apply, what activities produce the social value, what intermediate and final outcomes are expected, and over what timeframe? This is not complex — but it needs to be explicit and agreed before delivery starts, not reconstructed at the end.

**Baseline data.** Many of the SRMs measure change — from unemployment to employment, from no qualification to a recognised credential. Without a baseline, change cannot be measured. Where individual-level data is not feasible, proxy baselines using local area statistics (from the ONS, DWP, or the Office for Students) can be used, provided the method is transparent.

**Proportionate data collection.** The 37 SRMs require specific data points. Suppliers need a data collection plan — whether that is HR system extracts, participant records, or employer returns — agreed with the buyer before contract start. Retrospective data collection is expensive, unreliable, and rarely produces credible evidence.

**Attribution and plausibility.** For small and medium contracts, rigorous counterfactual analysis is rarely feasible. What is feasible is a contribution analysis: a structured argument that the programme's activities were a plausible cause of the outcomes observed, drawing on delivery data, participant feedback and what else was going on in the local area. This is proportionate, transparent, and defensible.

**Reporting to the Central Digital Platform.** For contracts of £5m or more, KPI performance must be published. Buyers should build reporting templates into the contract from the outset so that reporting is a routine administrative step, not a last-minute scramble.

## The January 2027 deadline and what to do now

If your organisation commences a covered procurement on or after 1 January 2027, PPN 026 applies. That means procurement teams running early-year contracts need to have their social value evaluation framework ready now — before the ITT is published — because the MACs, SRMs and KPI approach need to be in the specification, not bolted on after award.

For those designing bids, the key question is what social value your delivery model genuinely produces, and whether you have or can build the data infrastructure to evidence it. Vague aspirations score poorly under the new qualitative approach. Specific, locally-grounded commitments tied to plausible delivery mechanisms and clear SRM data points score well.

For independent evaluators commissioned to assess programme impact, the job now includes understanding PPN 026 obligations from the outset — because the data the supplier collects against SRMs is the primary evidence base for any subsequent impact assessment.
