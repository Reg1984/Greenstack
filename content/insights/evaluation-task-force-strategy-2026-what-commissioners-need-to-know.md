Two significant policy shifts landed this autumn that will change how evaluation is commissioned and used across the public sector. If you run programmes, fund interventions or manage public contracts, both are worth understanding before the new year.

## The Evaluation Task Force's 2026–2029 strategy

The [Evaluation Task Force](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluation-task-force-strategy-2026-2029/evaluation-task-force-strategy-2026-2029-html) (ETF) — a joint HM Treasury and Cabinet Office unit set up in 2021 — published its second strategy in June 2026. The headline shift is this: the ETF is moving from *making evaluation happen* to ensuring it *drives better outcomes*. That's not just a rebrand. It has real implications for what commissioners will be expected to produce and when.

The previous strategy built the infrastructure: the Magenta Book was revised, the Evaluation Registry was launched, and the ETF's own analysis found that the proportion of major government projects with good-quality evaluation plans rose from 8% in 2019 to 34% in 2025. That's meaningful progress, but 66% of major projects still lack adequate evaluation plans — which tells you something about the scale of the gap that remains.

The new strategy has four priority workstreams:

- **Cross-government evaluation** — integrating evidence across complex, multi-agency programmes, including place-based interventions
- **Governance and accountability** — embedding evaluation requirements into spending, departmental governance and accountability frameworks
- **Capability building** — training policy and project delivery professionals, and extending support to local government at Mayoral Strategic Authority (MSA) level
- **Innovation** — championing adaptive and "test and learn" evaluation, and building frameworks for evaluating AI and digital tools in public services

### What this means in practice

**Mandatory Evaluation Registry registration** is now a requirement, not a recommendation, for all planned central government evaluations. If you're designing a programme for a government department or local authority, expect your evaluation plan to be published on the Registry from the outset — and expect it to be scrutinised.

**The "translation gap" is now a named problem.** The ETF explicitly acknowledges that having more evaluations is not enough if findings don't change decisions. Commissioners should expect funders and departments to push harder for evaluations that are designed with use in mind — with clear decision points built into the design, not bolted on at the end.

**Local government is now firmly in scope.** The ETF's new strategy explicitly targets MSA-level capability. Combined authorities and mayoral regions commissioning evaluations of employment, housing, transport or economic development programmes should expect increased scrutiny of their evidence base, especially as they receive multi-year Spending Review settlements with delivery conditions attached.

**AI evaluation is a new frontier.** The ETF is developing frameworks for evaluating AI and digital tools in public services. Commissioners embedding AI into service delivery or back-office functions should start thinking now about how they'll demonstrate impact and learning.

## PPN 026: the new social value model for central government procurement

On 5 August 2026, the Cabinet Office published [Procurement Policy Note 026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ppn-026-the-social-value-model/ppn-026-the-social-value-model-html), which replaces the previous Social Value Model with effect from **1 January 2027** — applying to all in-scope procurements commenced on or after that date.

This is the second overhaul of the central government social value model in eighteen months. Here's what has changed:

| Feature | Previous model | PPN 026 (from Jan 2027) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Multiple themes (COVID, SMEs, net zero, etc.) | Two outcomes: Good Jobs and Skills |
| Minimum weighting | 10% for most contracts | 10% for £1m–£5m; **20% for £5m+** |
| KPIs | Voluntary | Mandatory for contracts of £5m or more |
| Enforcement | Limited | Poor KPI performance can bar future tenders |

The narrowing of the model to two outcomes — **Good Jobs** and **Skills** — with six award criteria (high quality jobs, fair working conditions, fair pay, training and retraining, in-work progression, and talent pipeline) is the most significant structural change. The previous model's broader social and environmental themes are gone from central government procurement.

The raised minimum weighting (20% for contracts of £5m+) matters enormously at scale. A Stotles analysis found that central government IT and software contracts worth £5m or more totalled £32.4bn in 2025/26 — the tier now scored at 20% social value. For suppliers bidding on large contracts, and for commissioners evaluating social value commitments, this raises the bar substantially.

The requirement for published social value KPIs on contracts of £5m or more — with underperformance potentially triggering exclusion from future tenders — also introduces a genuine accountability mechanism. This is new, and it shifts social value from a scoring exercise to an ongoing performance obligation.

Practitioner guidance, sub-criteria and the List of Community Programmes were due in autumn 2026. As of this article's publication date, that guidance is expected imminently and will add important detail on how to apply the six award criteria in practice.

## Three practical actions for commissioners right now

**1. Register planned evaluations early.** Whether you're a charity in receipt of government grant funding, a local authority managing a devolved programme, or a public body overseeing a major project, registering your evaluation plan on the Evaluation Registry early is increasingly expected — and increasingly visible to funders and oversight bodies.

**2. Build evaluation into programme design, not the final year.** The ETF's explicit focus on the "translation gap" signals that post-hoc evaluations submitted at the end of a programme cycle will face greater scrutiny. Adaptive designs, with clear decision points and feedback loops built in from the start, are what commissioners and funders are increasingly asking for.

**3. Re-map your social value approach before January 2027.** If your organisation bids for or manages central government contracts, the shift to the two-outcome PPN 026 model means that social value responses built around the old model's themes will need rebuilding. The six award criteria under Good Jobs and Skills are narrower and more specific — and will require different evidence, different community programme linkages, and (for larger contracts) measurable KPIs.

---

The ETF strategy and PPN 026 together signal the same direction of travel: more rigour, more transparency, and more consequence when evidence and social value are treated as compliance boxes rather than genuine tools for learning and improvement. For commissioners who take that seriously, the shift is an opportunity.
