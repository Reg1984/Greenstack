The Department for Education announced in January 2026 that it would invest £23 million in an AI tutoring programme, with the aim of supporting up to 450,000 disadvantaged pupils in Years 9–11. Trials began in secondary schools in autumn 2026. Full rollout is planned by the end of 2027. For multi-academy trusts, local authority-maintained schools and education charities, the question is no longer whether AI tutoring is coming — it's whether your school is ready to use it safely and well.

## What the government is actually doing

The DfE's programme is being developed jointly with i.AI (the Incubator for Artificial Intelligence). Eight suppliers — the AI Tutoring Pioneers — were selected through a procurement process and have been working with teachers since spring 2026 to co-design tools that meet DfE's safety and quality criteria. From September 2026, some of those suppliers have been partnered with additional schools to explore use across wider contexts.

The target group is children in receipt of free school meals in Years 9 to 11. The ambition is to give these pupils access to something closer to one-to-one tuition — the kind of personalised academic support that has historically been available mainly to those whose families can pay for it. That aspiration is grounded in a real and stubborn problem: at Key Stage 2, only 47% of disadvantaged pupils met the expected standard in reading, writing and maths combined in 2025, compared with 69% of their non-disadvantaged peers. By the end of secondary school, the attainment gap is estimated at more than 19 months. [The Tony Blair Institute for Global Change](https://institute.global/insights/public-services/generation-ready-scaling-safe-high-quality-ai-englands-schools) described England's education system as "at a crossroads" and argued that AI, used purposefully, could be a meaningful lever — though it was careful to stress that the causes of the gap are complex and technology is not a silver bullet.

Researchers and practitioners have broadly welcomed the direction of travel, while calling for more independent evidence. Schools Week reported that experts urged the DfE to commission pre-deployment efficacy research before scaling up. At the time of writing, no independent randomised evaluation of the specific pioneer tools has been published.

## What the DfE guidance says about AI in schools

Separate from the tutoring pilot, the DfE updated its guidance on the use of generative AI in schools and colleges in May 2026. The key principles are:

- **Human oversight is non-negotiable.** AI cannot replace the professional judgement and subject knowledge of teachers. Staff must check AI-generated output for accuracy, bias, age-appropriateness and curriculum fit before it reaches learners.
- **No personal data into consumer AI tools.** Staff must not enter pupil names, SEND information, attendance data or any other identifiable information into AI systems that have not been approved by the school or trust.
- **Age restrictions apply.** Many AI tools are restricted to users aged 13 or 18 and over. The DfE expects schools to comply with these restrictions and to make parents aware of relevant policies.
- **SEND and EAL learners need specific consideration.** Leaders must assess whether AI outputs might disadvantage particular groups, including pupils with special educational needs or those learning English as an additional language.

The updated guidance treats AI as "a managed education tool, not a shortcut around teacher responsibility." For governors and trustees, that framing matters: it places accountability squarely with the school, not with the technology provider.

## What every school needs: an AI policy

The DfE's guidance gives schools room to design their own AI policies rather than prescribing a single template, but it is not a blank cheque. A compliant, workable AI policy needs to cover at least the following:

| Area | Minimum requirement |
|---|---|
| Data protection | UK GDPR compliance; no personal or sensitive data in unapproved tools; DPIA where required |
| Safeguarding | Risk assessment for bias, misinformation and harmful outputs; alignment with current KCSIE |
| Academic integrity | Clear definitions of acceptable use for pupils; approach to assessment and verification |
| Staff training | Named lead; approved tools list; procedure for raising concerns |
| Parental communication | Transparent statement on how and when AI is used with pupils |
| SEND and EAL | Specific consideration of accessibility and potential bias for identified groups |

Multi-academy trusts should not leave this to individual schools. A trust-level policy framework, with school-level implementation, is both more efficient and more consistent with trustees' oversight responsibilities under the Academy Trust Handbook 2026, which strengthened expectations around SEND inclusion and governance.

## What 'good' AI tutoring actually looks like

Not all AI tutoring tools are equal. The evidence base, while growing, remains limited — particularly for secondary-age pupils in England. School leaders evaluating tools (whether as part of the DfE's programme or independently) should ask:

- **What does the evidence say?** Is there peer-reviewed research on this specific tool, or on tools with comparable design? Be cautious of vendors who cite AI's general potential rather than their product's specific impact.
- **Who designed it, and with whom?** Tools designed with teachers and trialled in real classrooms are more likely to be curriculum-appropriate and practically usable.
- **How does it handle SEND?** Does the tool adapt for pupils with dyslexia, processing difficulties or other learning needs, or does it assume a standard learner?
- **What data does it collect, and where does it go?** Understand the data model before any procurement decision. Cloud-based tools with offshore data processing may create compliance issues.
- **How does it fit into teaching, rather than replace it?** The most effective edtech supports a teacher's work — it doesn't create a parallel system the teacher doesn't understand or trust.

## The practical question for leaders

England's schools collectively spend approximately £900 million a year on educational technology, according to parliamentary evidence submitted in 2026. That is a significant investment, and much of it is not evaluated. The DfE's AI tutoring programme represents a moment of genuine policy intent to shift that — to build tools that are tested, teacher-designed and targeted at pupils who need support most.

But the rollout timeline means that 2026-27 is a preparation year for most schools. Trusts and local authority school improvement teams that use this year to build clear AI governance frameworks, train staff well and understand the evidence will be in a much stronger position when the pioneer tools become more widely available.

If your school or trust doesn't yet have a written AI policy, or hasn't reviewed it since the May 2026 DfE guidance update, that is the right place to start.
