School absence is not improving fast enough. That is the DfE's conclusion — and in September 2026, it responded by issuing every state-funded school in England with its own individual attendance target, backed by AI-generated data analysis and a new inspection judgement. For school leaders, governors and trust boards, the landscape has shifted. Here is what it means in practice.

## The scale of the problem

The post-pandemic attendance crisis has proved stubbornly persistent. The overall absence rate across all schools stood at **6.93% in 2025/26** — well above the pre-pandemic baseline of 4.73% in 2018/19. Persistent absence (missing 10% or more of sessions) affected nearly one in five pupils: 13.5% in primary schools and 24.3% in secondary schools.

The human cost is significant. A cohort study of 23,000 pupils by the Leeds Institute for Data Analytics found that [19% of persistently absent pupils went on to become persistently NEET](https://www.centreforsocialjustice.org.uk/newsroom/school-absence-crisis-now-entrenched-new-data-reveals) (not in education, employment or training) aged 16 to 18. The Centre for Social Justice estimates that, without sustained action, almost 180,000 pupils will leave school during this Parliament and become NEET, partly as a result of persistent absence — at an estimated cost to the Exchequer of £125 billion per year for all NEETs combined.

The DfE noted that 2024/25 saw "the biggest improvement in attendance in a decade", with 5.3 million more days attended and 140,000 fewer persistently absent pupils. But it also found that more than a third of schools showed no improvement at all — which is what prompted the new targets.

## What is an ABIE?

An **Attendance Baseline Improvement Expectation (ABIE)** is a school-specific minimum attendance improvement target set by the DfE. Unlike a blanket national threshold, each ABIE is tailored to the school's context using factors including:

- historic attendance trends
- location and regional patterns
- pupil needs and the proportion of pupils with SEND
- levels of deprivation in the school's catchment

Two schools with similar overall absence rates may receive different improvement expectations if their circumstances differ. The DfE uses AI-driven modelling to generate the targets and benchmarks each school against a group of similar schools — the "similar schools" report within the [Monitor Your School Attendance portal](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/monitor-your-school-attendance-service).

The ABIE is **not a sanction trigger**. Missing the target does not lead to formal accountability action. Instead, it signals to the DfE's new Regional Improvement for Standards and Excellence (RISE) teams that support may be needed — including reviewing attendance processes, modelling pupil-level data, linking the school with high-performing peers, and helping schools work more effectively with local services.

Crucially, ABIEs are not published publicly. Parents will not see their school's target. But governors and trustees will be expected to know and act on them.

## The troubled rollout — and where things stand now

It is worth acknowledging that the ABIE rollout had a difficult start. When the DfE announced the initiative in November 2025, it said schools would receive targets "from this month." Days later, the targets were suspended due to data quality issues. By February 2026, [survey data from Teacher Tapp](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/schools-have-not-got-dfe-ai-attendance-targets) indicated that nearly half of headteachers had not received an ABIE or did not know whether they had.

The DfE described 2025/26 as an "indicative year" and committed to providing all schools with an official ABIE from September 2026. That rollout is now under way. If your school has not yet located its ABIE, check the Monitor Your School Attendance portal — it is available there, alongside the AI-generated similar schools report.

## What Ofsted will look for from September 2026

The inspection stakes have risen. From September 2026, attendance sits within a dedicated **Attendance and Behaviour judgement** under the updated Ofsted inspection toolkit. This is a standalone judgement — not subsumed into leadership or personal development.

Inspectors will examine:

- whether leaders prioritise improving attendance for **all** absence categories — occasional, persistent and severe
- whether the school communicates expectations clearly and consistently to families
- whether staff act promptly when attendance starts to dip, rather than waiting for it to become a persistent problem
- whether registers are accurate and day-to-day follow-up is systematic
- whether leaders use **pupil-level data analysis** to identify patterns and root causes, not just overall figures
- whether the school works effectively with parents, local authorities and other agencies for vulnerable groups

The governance test is equally important. [Browne Jacobson's analysis of the new inspection toolkit](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/school-attendance-guidance-2026-clarification-not-a-legal-reset) notes that inspectors will test whether trust boards and governing boards are exercising **genuine oversight** — reviewing trends by pupil group and by day of the week, challenging leaders on what they find, and ensuring that scrutiny actively influences intervention. Receiving a single attendance figure once a term will not be sufficient.

## What school leaders and trust boards should do this term

### 1. Access your ABIE and similar schools report

Log in to the Monitor Your School Attendance portal. Find your ABIE and, critically, identify which schools the DfE is benchmarking you against. These peer schools are worth contacting — the DfE has said it will use AI to link struggling schools with high-performing peers, but proactive contact is faster.

### 2. Break absence data down by pupil group

Overall absence rates obscure the most important patterns. Analyse by: year group, day of the week (Monday and Friday spikes are common), pupil premium, SEND status, ethnicity, and type of absence (authorised vs unauthorised). Which groups are driving persistent absence in your school? The answers will shape your intervention.

### 3. Distinguish causes — and responses

Persistent absence has multiple root causes, and effective responses are cause-specific. The CSJ's research identifies three overarching drivers: **a breakdown of trust between home and school**, **deteriorating educational ambition**, and **a school offer that fails to engage** some groups of pupils. SEND, mental health, and domestic instability are frequently co-occurring factors. Blanket attendance policies and fines are most effective for occasional absence; they have limited impact on the families of persistently absent pupils, who typically face more complex barriers.

### 4. Strengthen family relationships before crises hit

The most effective attendance work happens before absence becomes entrenched. Schools with strong home-school relationships — regular, non-punitive contact; named family support workers; accessible pastoral routes — typically see better outcomes than those relying primarily on penalty notices. The July 2026 edition of the [DfE's statutory attendance guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a4f9ec6a6586e258d371bd0/Working_together_to_improve_school_attendance_2026.pdf) is explicit that schools, local authorities and families must work together — with schools identifying in-school barriers and local authorities supporting families with wider issues.

### 5. Update governor and trustee oversight

Boards need to move from termly attendance figures to **regular, granular review**. A good attendance standing item at governor and trustee meetings should include: overall and persistent absence rates compared to previous terms and to the ABIE; trends by pupil group; what interventions are in place and what evidence of impact exists; and what the RISE team has flagged or supported.

## A note on SEND and attendance

There is a significant and often under-examined overlap between SEND and attendance. Many persistently absent pupils have unmet or unidentified SEND needs — including anxiety, autism, ADHD, and processing difficulties — that make the school day itself a barrier. Schools implementing their inclusion strategies under the Inclusive Mainstream Fund are well placed to address this systematically: Principle 5 of the DfE's inclusion framework explicitly covers safe culture, belonging and attendance, and IMF spending can fund the relational and pastoral approaches that move the dial on this cohort.

For trust leaders managing both the ABIE process and the inclusion strategy deadline of 31 December 2026, there is a real opportunity to treat these as a unified workstream rather than two separate compliance tasks.

## The bigger picture

The ABIE system represents a shift in how the DfE uses data. AI-generated, school-specific targets are a step towards a more granular, contextually sensitive accountability model — and that is broadly welcome. The critical challenge is that many of the factors driving persistent absence are, as school leaders have pointed out, [beyond any individual school's direct control](https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/news/schools-ai-powered-student-attendance-targets-absence): housing instability, domestic difficulties, mental health crises, and unmet SEND needs all require cross-agency responses that schools alone cannot deliver.

The RISE teams, local authority attendance functions, and voluntary sector partners all have a role to play. The schools and trusts most likely to move their ABIE in a positive direction are those that have mapped their absence data carefully, built genuine partnerships with families and local services, and created an in-school culture where pupils want to come.
